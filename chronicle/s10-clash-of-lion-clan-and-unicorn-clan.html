Clash of Lion Clan and Unicorn Clan

Session 10·2025-05-27

Three hundred years before any of this, on ground that is now a line in a disputed deed, the Lion held a war council in the snow. Two officers argued against crossing the bog. The commander heard them out and went anyway.

The Lion Clan attack was supposed to go in at first light and did not, because the army was not yet marshalled. Kitsu Somalia, Akuyaku and Toronoko bought the delay — Toronoko by taking a rival's own argument away from him in front of the first council and handing it back inverted. What the scouts brought in with the dawn was worth the price: the Unicorn camp was not asleep. It was dug in — earthworks, stakes, watchtowers facing every direction, archers already placed, and their own scouts working the ground. The extent of the fortification says they have been there a while.

The ground was worse than the camp. The field the Lion Clan meant to cross is bog with fresh snow lying on thick mud: ensnaring, not impossible, and survivable if a man is warned.

And there were not many horses.

That last is where the council went wrong, and it went wrong through Matsu Morozane. He had the observation and he chose to sell it rather than report it: abandoning many horses appears cowardly, and an enemy so ill-prepared as to leave them behind is an enemy already beaten. He pitched it well and he pitched it badly, which is to say he failed the room and entertained it instead. The Lion commanders guffawed. What fools. Matsu Sakura wanted to believe it, which is exactly why it should have been put to her straight.

What the scouts could actually support was thinner. They cannot see into that camp. They cannot say whether the horses were ever there or have been moved, because establishing that needs boots inside the perimeter, and the only man who could do it is Akuyaku, and it would cost a night. There are not enough horses visible for a full cavalry force. There are enough for outriders and counter-scouts, which is precisely what has been keeping the Lion scouts from ranging any closer.

Kitsu Somalia did not laugh. The spirits said the Unicorn had prepared the entire field and were waiting for the Lion to trip their defences and attack them on ground of their own choosing. They seek to counter patience with patience. She proposed settling it by a contest of champions, which is honourable, cheap and available, and which landed as presumptuous rather than absurd. Matsu Sakura refused it as premature — later, perhaps, if it came to that. There is no point bringing an army to a duel to just have a duel.

Akuyaku argued for time. Time for the troops, time for the ground, time to find out why the Unicorn had spent three or four days preparing a battlefield and what they had done to it. Pushing the line of control forward was what he actually wanted, so his scouts could range far enough to bring back something reliable — and he wanted a way in from the east, through the village and up into the mountains where the Lion maps stop. What he could see from here was that the terrain gets rockier going up, and that he could guess at the rest and not know it.

Matsu Sakura did not simply overrule them. She put the question to the room properly. Some of you want caution, and some of you agree with me that there needs to be some testing of their mettle. I trust my advisors. If you think there is good reason not to give them battle today, I want to know what we gain by not doing so.

Nobody could put a number on it. Akuyaku's honest answer was if you are set on attacking, attack — and then the two questions worth asking, which are what intelligence a day actually buys and how it makes tomorrow's victory likelier. Kitsu Somalia gave the sharpest version of the argument anyone managed: when your enemy has a trap prepared for you, it would be in poor taste not to spring it — but spring it in a manner of your choosing. The trouble is that nobody in that tent could say what the trap was. They had a partial idea. They did not have a complete one.

Matsu Hisoka — Matsu Sakura's husband, Akodo-born and a graduate of the Akodo War College — pointed out that deploying for battle would draw a response. Matsu Morozane answered that the Unicorn had not attacked while the Lion were making camp, at their most vulnerable, and so would not attack a line moving forward either: they have the advantage and they intend to keep sitting on it. Akuyaku got close enough to contempt that Toronoko put a hand on his shoulder — I suggest taking a step back before you speak in a way that will lead to misunderstandings.

The compromise was Akuyaku's own. Let the army deploy across the field and test them; use the noise of it as cover; and put him into their camp under it. Not through the water in winter — around by the boulders on the western side, up from the north end of the rice paddies, hugging what cover the winter leaves, and moving once it is dark. The muster would be his signal. He leaves at sundown.

So the battle line would go across the bog to find out what the Unicorn were made of, and Toronoko, Kitsu Somalia and Matsu Morozane would take unit commands and take them forward, under the snow, toward a camp that had chosen to stand on this ground and was waiting on it.

Setsuna

This is not a session she attended. It is a memory she is inside, three centuries deep, looking out through Matsu Morozane's eyes at the battle the diamond mines case turns on. She has all of his thoughts and he has the reins; taking them from him costs her something every time she does it, and she did not do it here.

She watched her own ancestor tell a general what the general wanted to hear. The horses were the one hard fact the Lion had and nobody could interpret, and Matsu Morozane dressed it as Unicorn cowardice because that is what played in a Lion tent. It got a laugh. It did not get a decision. Whatever else the record of this battle says, the Lion went in believing something no scout had established.

What the flashback establishes that the Hall of Scribes does not is the preparation. The Unicorn had days on that field — works, stakes, towers, archers — and the Lion knew it and crossed anyway. Neither side chose the ground alone: the Lion came under orders to take it, found the Unicorn already standing on it, and neither would be the first to move. Ikoma Ichigo's account gives the Unicorn the victory. It does not give them the earthworks, and a victory that was prepared for is a different fact in a deed dispute than a victory that was won on the day.

And she should hold on to what the council never says. They argue that ground for a whole evening — the bog, the snow, the rice paddies, the boulders, the eastern village, the mountains where the maps run out — and the Old Diamond Mines are never mentioned once by anybody. Whether that is because neither army knew, or because neither thought them worth a word, this record cannot tell her. Either answer is worth having.

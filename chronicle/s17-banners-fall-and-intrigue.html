Banner's Fall and Intrigue

Session 17·2025-07-21

The Emperor's horns came over the field with both armies still standing in each other. Nothing on the Snow Plain was decided. It was stopped.

Shinjo Kamo spent everything he had left in one push and the battle stopped being manoeuvre and became grinding. Discipline went on both sides. Akodo Toronoko met him in a duel in the middle of it, and the two of them went at each other, went down, and got up to do it again.

Matsu Morozane was looking at the horse. Shinjo Kamo's banner was fixed to his saddle — the standard the whole Unicorn line took its bearing from — and a banner on a horse can be taken if you are willing to take the horse. He put his soldiers around it with spears up to make the animal think about them, rode in on Shiguro Chinmoku while it was distracted, got a hand on the reins and hauled, and threw Shinjo Kamo into the snow. The cold ground broke the general's fall. Then he had the banner off the saddle and up over his head where his own general could see it.

That is the blow the Lion Clan had been trying to land for a week, and it is not a wound. It is a symbol taken off a horse in front of an army, and the panic it puts through a line is worth more than a cohort.

Then the horns went. Riders came over the field under the chrysanthemum, heralds of The Emperor, and everything stopped being about the ground.

Kitsu Somalia was found in the snow — severely wounded, still alive. Kitsu Yui got to him and dragged him out of it, and the heralds' arrival broke her concentration at exactly the wrong moment; he surfaced, said nothing useful, and went under again. Before dawn the Unicorn came in one more time with fire arrows and screaming arrows, and it went nowhere: two lines locked against each other, bleeding, neither able to move the other.

In the confusion something happened that nobody came out of well. Akodo Toronoko's own husband was nearly gutted, and by the account carried out of that field it was her hand that did it. Blame went looking for one person to land on and found one.

An emissary of the Imperial Houses was on the field now, which meant judgement on this whole war was coming whether either clan wanted it. And then Kitsu Somalia came round and said the thing that mattered more than the banner: he had heard his would-be killer's voice, and he knew it. A woman. I would recognize the voice because it's not the first time she's patched me up.

The spy was one of their own healers. And Kitsu Somalia was the only man alive who could put a name to her.

Setsuna

The Snow Plain was never decided. It was interrupted. Two armies locked in a stalemate, an Imperial emissary on the field, and the Emperor's judgement arriving over the top of both of them. Nobody won that ground. Somebody was later awarded it, and the difference between those two verbs is the entire dispute she has been sent to weigh.

That reframes the deed. If the mines passed by Imperial judgement rather than by conquest, then the question is not who held the hill in 1123-minus-two-hundred, but what the Empire was told when it ruled — and the Empire was told by the same clans whose accounts she has already found to be silent about the mines' existence. An Imperial ruling made in ignorance of the property's value is a very different document from one made with knowledge of it.

Two other things to carry out of the memory. The Lion's spy was a healer in their own camp, a woman whose voice Kitsu Somalia knew — and he was the only one who could name her, and he was unconscious more than he was awake. Whether he ever did name her is not in this memory.

And Matsu Morozane took an enemy general's banner off his saddle in front of both armies. Her own ancestor did that. It is the sort of thing a family remembers and a historian does not, and it is worth knowing whether the Hall of Scribes records it — because if the Lion account omits even their own trophies, the omissions are not selective, they are systemic.

Court of Ancestral Shadows

Session 26·2025-10-20

Monban summoned two ancestors to prove the Scorpion had helped — blood of his mother and blood of his father. The first thing they did was look at each other with disgust and call each other liars.

Kitsuki Kaage put the obvious question to the room: how does anyone attest to events three hundred years before they were born? The answer was that they had ridden their own ancestors through the battle, and he liked it no better for being explained. A small mousy voice came from the back corner — Kitsu Ayoko, whom he had been told was only there to present evidence from the library. We were not sure you would allow even this testimony, my lord.

She was allowed to speak, and what she said turned the case. Her ancestor is Kitsu Yue, and Kitsu Yue felt terrible guilt over her part in the battle: that she had won it for the Lion by means she thought unfair. And toward the end of the fighting she did something that caused the histories to be lost, and felt horrible about it, and wanted the record corrected. So she sought out a descendant to be the engine of her delivery. The scroll Kitsu Yue meant her to find is what put the whole party into the vision — and it has been opened since, and does nothing. It worked once.

Kitsuki Kaage's face went to the look a man wears listening to a fairy tale. Are you sure you did not inhale some mushroom spores and have a strange vision?

They held. Kakita Kazumi pointed out that all of them had experienced the same battle. They saw it from separate places in it, so when the vision divided them into different parts of the field what each one saw stayed consistent with the others — and they have checked. When they were together they saw the same people: Shinjō Kamo, Matsu Sakura. And they saw more than the fighting — travel, conversations, the letter, the treaty. Some versions of it, in fact. Almost the same. Somewhat suspicious. One of them offered to walk the court to the spot and show where the papers were hidden.

Kitsuki Kaage noted, with interest, that the letter had come from Akodo Akihito — another of his students — and allowed the testimony, with every witness open to examination by both advocates.

Doji Setsuna went first and did not pretend this was where her case lived. She gave a plain account of Matsu Morozane's battle: the poisonings, the weather, the unnatural, the surprises in the mountains, and the Lion holding against all of it — until the Emperor's people arrived and ended the matter by decree rather than by arms.

Shosuro Aishi worked her, and got precision instead of advantage. Were the Lion set to triumph? Too strong. It was leading their direction, despite heavy odds. Were they favoured? The edge was to the Lion, but it was not a decisive one. They had killed four of the five pillars. Did Matsu Morozane see the Unicorn taking Scorpion help? Morozane's eyes caught no direct evidence of it.

Then how, Shosuro Aishi asked, were the Unicorn waiting for the Lion pincer on the second day? And here Doji Setsuna gave the best answer of her day, which was to concede the gap and close it at the same time: she could not account for everything, and had only one man's eyes to see through, and he saw nothing that implicated the Scorpion — and if it had been the Scorpion, would it not have been done in a way that implicated nobody? That is my understanding of how ideal infiltrations work. But I can only speak to what was seen and heard through the eyes I was looking through.

It cut both ways, as precise testimony does. Ide Tsubame took it to mean the Unicorn could have triumphed without Scorpion assistance, and moved again for dismissal — the whole claim rests on a few lines in a letter and a handful of forgeries. Shosuro Aishi took the same testimony the other way: Morozane's own account puts a Scorpion at the first clash and a Scorpion at the treaty signing, and presence is what the claim needs. Asked to explain the Scorpion at the signing, Doji Setsuna said Morozane's mind held no explanation — he was profoundly courageous and did not weigh his mind down with hypotheses about things that were not warfare.

Ide Tsubame could not help herself and offered, out of turn, that Shinjō Kamo was simply a good tactician who knew to block the routes to his own camp. Kitsuki Kaage told her it was not her time — and this is why I want advocates here, because otherwise I would be talking to myself.

Doji Setsuna's testimony counted as support for the Unicorn, and Ide Tsubame gained ground with it. Shosuro Aishi's rebuttal, unassisted and against a judge with no appetite for ghost stories, gained none at all, and Kitsuki Kaage looked at her dismissively.

Kakita Kazumi testified next: the two clans were matched in valour, the combined journals tell one story, the Scorpion may have tried to tilt the battle and did not succeed, the Lion should have won, and the field would still be contested to this day — except that the Emperor stopped it. So the Emperor should have the benefit, as the cost of the opportunity he took away — the mines into Imperial custody.

What nobody in the room saw was that Doji Setsuna built the stage he stood on. Moving with the authority of her office, organising witnesses, closing off distractions and interruptions so that the attention of the chamber rested on one man, she cleared his path — and did it so completely that her involvement was invisible. Seven successes' worth of invisible. He came out of it with five bonus successes and used them flashily, and the court saw a Crane making a fine argument on his own.

Shiba Midori testified to the shugenja general she had ridden and the infiltrator from her own side who tried to kill her and was never seen again — someone inside the camp damaging the Lion's odds. And Kitsu Somalia had said that were it not for dishonourable tactics the Lion would surely have won.

Shosuro Aishi pounced on it, because it is the Scorpion's whole case in the mouth of a Phoenix magistrate: without the spy, the Lion might have won; therefore the Scorpion were assisting; therefore the Unicorn owe, and should pay in the land they promised. Let them keep the rest of the Snow Plain and freeze on it — the Scorpion want the output of the mines.

Shiba Midori answered that claiming the infiltrator now presumes a claim never made while he lived, and that her visions showed no Scorpion marking or any other. Shosuro Aishi smiled at her the way a parent smiles at a child — an old magistrate who does not understand that a spy does not wear the colours of the clan he comes from, how delightful — and then sharpened it: there is no proof this was the Scorpion's spy at all. Ide Tsubame caught the opening and rode it: the Unicorn certainly had their own counterintelligence, as the Lion do and will not admit, and the infiltrator could as easily have been theirs. Shosuro Aishi answered that no Unicorn would have been that good — the saboteur was not merely effective but undetected.

She took the Earth approach, which is the one Kitsuki Kaage favours, and gained ground with it. Ide Tsubame's counter — that a spy who failed to guarantee defeat is a spy who failed — made no progress at all. And at that point the tally had quietly turned over: there was now more weight behind ruling for the Empire than for the Scorpion.

Bayushi Monban rose to a courteous invitation and delivered ten points. Point one: the pincer manoeuvre failed, almost as if the Unicorn knew it was happening. And Ikoma Akuyaku told them. Ikoma Akuyaku went out scouting and came back a day late with his foot bound — bound by the Unicorn, for the information he had given them. The city fell as though someone had explained how best to surround the Lion. A third of the Lion host was poisoned out of the battle, which bought the Imperial Houses the time they needed. The scouts were killed for their intelligence, by Ikoma Akuyaku. The histories went missing from a burning tent, taken by Ikoma Akuyaku and delivered to the Scorpion — proved by their being found with the Scorpion letter. There were the letter, the histories and the draft treaty together. The Scorpion may not have stood at the signing, but the death of the Shinjō stopped any further dialogue between the Unicorn commander and the Imperials in which the Scorpion might have pressed the claim at once. Both Shiba Midori's ancestor and Doji Setsuna's were put out of action for more than one engagement, while Kakita Kazumi's cut down three or four of the pillars and the Shinjō after being blinded — and Ikoma Akuyaku had offered to be the Shinjō's chosen champion, and the man foolishly declined, and it cost him his life.

And last, the point the other nine were built to reach: if the object was to assist toward victory, victory was never granted on the battlefield at all. It was granted by giving the Unicorn time — time the Imperials used to arrive, stop the fighting, and name them victors. The battle standard of the Snow Plain hangs in this castle. The Lion kept the standard and were declared to have lost the war.

The Scorpion assisted the Unicorn. The Unicorn agreed to pay with the mines. What is earned is owed. What is owed is paid.

Then Ide Tsubame observed that he knew all this because he had hallucinated his ancestor, and he stopped arguing the case and went after the clan. Their people made a deal and are squirming out of it; all they brought was paper proving their lord was a sloppy writer; in the Empire an agreement is clean and precise and elegant, and they would know that if they had not ridden off into the desert for two centuries. And now they are under the Lion's claws again, and the Dragon will not help them, and the Phoenix do not trust them, and the Crane have been ordered away from the Lion by the Emperor, and the Crab have their own troubles — and the only neighbours who might help are the Scorpion, again, who will not, because the Unicorn cannot and will not pay what they owe, and use the Empire's own laws and its Bushidō to paper over a clan's failures.

It was magnificent and it failed. Kitsuki Kaage is not a man moved by emotional flourish, and the roll came apart. Bayushi Monban spent what he had left putting strife on Ide Tsubame and on the judge.

Shiba Midori then made the argument nobody else was making. The ancestors have wishes for the society they left: the Lion spirits believed that defending the Lion claim served Rokugan, and Kitsu Yue believed that lending her descendant her eyes would right an injustice. And Shiba Midori had been shown another injustice — Kitsu Yue's part in recovering certain Kitsu shugenja techniques was credited to Kitsu Somalia instead, partly from Kitsu Yue's own timidity and for no other good reason, when her courage and her mastery of the kami should have stood on their own. And therefore: to let the Scorpion admit methods that would be called dishonourable in any other forum, and then pay them for it, sets a precedent, and this ruling will shape every ruling after it.

Shosuro Aishi demanded to know who she was to dictate what is honourable in war. I am not dictating. I am reminding. I am a magistrate just as much as you — except you got to talk and I did not. Kitsuki Kaage made the advocate withdraw it, and then shut both advocates and the other magistrates out of the exchange entirely so that Shiba Midori could finish.

She finished, and it did not land. There was polite silence for a moment, and the court moved on. What she took from it was smaller and more private: that she is genuinely offended by the Scorpion's lack of righteousness, and that righteousness in the people around her matters to her more than she had known — which is, as it happens, the virtue of her own clan.

Then Kitsuki Kaage asked Kitsu Ayoko what she had to say, and she went white as a sheet. She had not expected to be asked. Doji Setsuna steadied her without a word, on poise alone, and Shiba Midori shored her up as well, and the girl spoke.

My ancestor made a mistake because they were afraid. Afraid that she had nearly won the battle for the Lion by means she thought dishonourable, and unready to take responsibility for her own choices. She believed she was protecting someone greater than herself in Kitsu Somalia. She did not recognise her own strength, or that her contribution might have been worth a great deal. The ancestors favoured them, the ancestors respected them, and the ancestors answered their call. It is said only the most honourable and true among the spirit speakers can call the ancestors to fight on their behalf. It is my destiny. I will be worthy of this power one day, as she was.

And then the finding: Kitsu Yue was the hero of the Battle of the Snow Plain for the Lion, and the histories should have said so, and neither Unicorn valour nor Scorpion treachery could touch her honour or make her fail. She was compromised by the time she got it out, and she got it out.

Bayushi Monban went at her anyway. Where does this evidence sit? In the official histories — the ones her clan thought lost, thought destroyed, and which the Scorpion liberated and kept safe for three centuries. Told they had been kept to be exploited, he asked how: if the Scorpion had them all that time they could have produced them as evidence; they are available now, and available because of the Scorpion. A clan made a deal with a clan, and that should be upheld first, because what are we without our word?

Then he found the seam. Her ancestor was afraid — is fear not against Bushidō, against being samurai? The answer came back with a citation: he had clearly not read Akodo's Leadership with any care. Fear is natural. Fear is in every soldier's heart. Going forward anyway is the essence of Bushidō.

Spoken like a true Lion — a Lion of the clan that lost the Snow Plain. And had she forgotten that her ancestor was ordered to hold with her unit, and broke away to do these celebrated things? Disobedience to a superior; disobedience to orders. Do not speak to me of honour on the battlefield. This case is about what is owed. The Unicorn were declared the winners and the Unicorn agreed to pay the Scorpion in mines to be made the winners, and they were made the winners.

She held her ground — that the battle was undecided, that the Scorpion's efforts had failed, that the Unicorn were named victors rather than winning. He told her that until these histories surfaced her ancestor was junk and looked down upon and her family left out in the cold, and that puffing up like a Lion would get nothing out of him. And then, quietly, that a magistrate saying such things would be a different matter — but as for her, he had already shown her testimony was unnecessary. But I certainly will not forget it.

Kitsuki Kaage ended it there and called for closing arguments — unless anyone had further evidence. If someone wants to, I do not know, summon an ancestor spirit, now would be the time.

Bayushi Monban took it: he would ask Kitsu Takeko to call up the Shosuro who wrote the letter and the treaty, and Ikoma Akuyaku.

Doji Setsuna unmasked. She broke decorum to interject — she would speak for the magistrates, there is no need for this, their testimony should be sufficient — and it was close to pleading, and it cost her for speaking out of turn. Kitsuki Kaage had not recognised her. I indicated I was willing to let you discuss your bizarre experiences. Why should I not entertain this? She asked to be excused from that portion of the proceedings, and was refused outright.

The advocates fought over it properly. Ide Tsubame turned the Scorpion's own jurisprudence on them: it is the Scorpion who have argued, successfully and repeatedly in the courts of this land, that a spirit is subject to the shugenja who summons it and can be made to say anything — so the precedent stands against summoning anything to answer for anything. Shosuro Aishi would not object on conditions: that the Kitsu daimyō testify to what the spirits say and an Emerald Magistrate who is herself a shugenja attest to what is seen. Shiba Midori added that there are three shugenja present, so no one of them could bend the spirits. And Bayushi Monban closed the argument for his own proposal: not a Scorpion, not a Unicorn, not one of the magistrates, but a disinterested third party — and he is the blood of both the dead in question, and there are now letters in their own hands to call them by.

I have allowed this farce to go on as long as I have. Why not. I will humour the matter.

Kitsu Takeko came forward. I am going to do my very best to see that these ancestors attend this matter. We shall see if they are willing to hear us, for they are long dead and have seen little reverence. Shiba Midori assisted her. It was not close — the dice came up in a heap, and the spirits did not stand a chance.

She threw something like salts into the braziers lighting the room. The flames went green, and the dead were visible.

Doji Setsuna held her composure by main force and controlled breathing and took the strife for it, and said aloud what she thought of a court that permits a séance when there are rulings and laws against it. Kitsuki Kaage unmasked to answer her. Because at this point this has gone to absolute madness, and I do not see why not to continue with it until it is concluded. It will not be one of the rulings he is remembered well for, and he knows it.

Then Bayushi Monban introduced the witnesses, and the room learned something about him. Blood of my mother, Shosuro Amane. Blood of my father, Ikoma Akuyaku.

Kitsu Takeko could make them seen but not heard, except by those spiritually inclined, and so she translated. The two spirits had looked at one another with open disgust. Shosuro Amane — who reminded Doji Setsuna very much of her descendant Shosuro Aishi, the same haughty carriage — said that Ikoma Akuyaku is the most accomplished liar she has ever had the misfortune to work with, and that nothing he says should be believed. Ikoma Akuyaku said she was simply disappointed not to be as capable an infiltrator as he is.

Setsuna

Her yojimbo is the descendant of the man the whole case turns on. Bayushi Monban stood up in an Imperial arbitration and named Shosuro Amane as his mother's blood and Ikoma Akuyaku as his father's. Every irregularity he listed for the Scorpion — the pincer betrayed, the bound foot, the poisoned host, the murdered scouts, the stolen histories — is a list of his own great-grandfather's crimes, recited with pride, as the proof of a debt. And the letter condemning the least of the Bayushi was written by his own ancestor's associate about a man his own ancestor had chosen. This is not a lawyer arguing a claim. This is a man collecting on his family.

And the moment the dead arrived, his case began eating itself. He summoned two witnesses to establish that the Scorpion had helped the Unicorn, and the first thing they did was look at each other with disgust, and the first thing said in that courtroom by a Scorpion of the Snow Plain was that Ikoma Akuyaku is an accomplished liar whose word is worth nothing. Bayushi Monban's entire documentary case rests on Ikoma Akuyaku's journals and Ikoma Akuyaku's confessions. His own ancestor has just impeached his own evidence, in front of the magistrate, unprompted. Ide Tsubame does not need to prove the forgery any more. She needs a transcript.

The thing she did best today, nobody saw, and that is the lesson. She built the whole room for Kakita Kazumi's argument — moved the witnesses, closed the gaps, killed the interruptions — and did it so cleanly that her hand was invisible and he came out of it five successes ahead. She also steadied Kitsu Ayoko into the best testimony of the session on nothing but composure. Compare that with what happened when she spoke in her own voice: broke decorum, near-pleaded, was not recognised, was denied, and paid for it. The influence she has in that chamber is not rhetorical, and the sooner she stops arguing and starts arranging, the better this goes.

Being refused the exit was the right ruling and she should be glad of it. She is also not alone in that room in the way she fears she is: Matsu Tomoe has already told her she walks beside her and is watching, and a séance is the one proceeding where that stops being a comfort and starts being a fact with consequences. What she wanted to avoid is precisely where the case is now decided, and a magistrate who walks out of the room when the evidence gets strange is a magistrate whose findings can be questioned later. She stayed, she held, and she took the strife. That is the record she wants.

The other thing to hold onto: the tally has turned. There is now more weight behind the Imperial finding than behind the Scorpion, and it got there from Shiba Midori's work rather than anyone's oratory. Kakita Kazumi's framing is the one to keep — the Emperor stopped the battle, so the Emperor bears the cost of what he took away, and the mines are the cost. That argument does not require anyone to prove a forgery, or to decide who was honourable three centuries ago, or to call an Emerald Magistrate a criminal. It only requires the court to notice who ended the fight.

And Kitsu Ayoko gave her the moral ground for it without being asked. If Kitsu Yue is the honour of the Snow Plain — and a Kitsu spirit-speaker saying so from the floor, while compromised, is worth more than any advocate saying it — then the honour of that battle belongs to a woman whose own clan lost, whose credit was given to someone else, and who is claimed by neither party to this suit. Nobody at that table can spend her. That is exactly the kind of fact an Imperial ruling is built on.

One quieter matter. Bayushi Monban told a frightened girl in open court that he would not forget what she said, and distinguished her from a magistrate who might have said the same. He has spent this week being humiliated by a letter, denounced by a Crane, and accused of forgery twice, and the person he chose to threaten was the youngest and least protected person in the room. That is worth remembering about him, separately from whatever he is owed.

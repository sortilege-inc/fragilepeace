Historical Intrigue and Dispute

Session 18·2025-08-26

Every account in the archive is genuine, in the right hand, in the right voice, consistent with the battle she lived — and every one of them leaves the Scorpion out. Nobody rewrote the history of the Snow Plain. They simply agreed not to write part of it down.

Three days until Kitsuki Kaage reaches the Castle of the Swift Sword with the Scorpion and Unicorn ambassadors behind him. The Scorpion have produced records claiming the diamond mines on the Snow Plain were deeded to them for services rendered to the Unicorn; the Unicorn deny it outright and say the ground is theirs because it is in their territory. The Lion claim nothing and are, for once, impartial. Kitsuki Kaage will rule. Nobody in this party will — they can only convince him.

Three days, and an archive, and four people who have just spent a week inside the battle in question.

The first thing the archive gave them was the shape of a murder. Ikoma Hideri was a well-regarded historian with exactly one failure on her name: the Battle of the Snow Plain went completely unrecorded. Nobody ever knew why. Most assumed she had died in it. What the party know is that she came off that field alive and was killed before she could finish writing it down, and that everything the Empire has about the battle is a reassembly from other people's fragments.

They read their own ancestors. Matsu Morozane had an illustrious career before and after and made extensive observations of many battles — he ended as an instructor at the Akodo War College — and his account of the Snow Plain is thin and sour. What is in it is not fighting. It is his frustration that the battle was decided by weather and supply and terrain and the lack of intelligence rather than by the strength of anyone's sword arm, as it should have been. Other military histories mention the Lion's provisioning difficulties too. That is the thread.

Kitsu Somalia left a great deal, and almost none of it about this. She wrote extensively about the technique by which a Kitsu can call the ancestors of the Lion into a battle — and never named where it came from. The official histories of the clan credit her with being the first to demonstrate it, in a different battle later in her career, and count her among the founding voices of the school that teaches it. About the Snow Plain she says very little, about the mines nothing at all, and in passing she mentions one particularly miserable trek up through the mountains, and discovering secrets there.

Kakita Kazumi copied everything the others found into one document and set the accounts side by side. Akodo Toronoko's narrative is proud of her own valour, reserves a handful of lines for the battle, and spends some of them defending Ikoma Akuyaku — vehemently, and to her dying day — against the charge that he was the source of the supply disaster. The enemy, she insists, simply predicted their movements too well. She regrets only that having beaten four of the five Pillars and their leader she was not the one to kill all five. She too ended at the Akodo War College.

And the hand changes. Everything Akodo Toronoko wrote before the Snow Plain is in her own hand. The record of the battle itself, and every record after it, is in somebody else's.

Matsu Sakura's account of the most famous engagement of her life is one sentence. Despite the brilliance of my generals and the valour of my soldiers, I feel that the winter stole our victory from us. She praises all of them, Akodo Toronoko and Matsu Morozane particularly, and allows that even Akodo Kagekatsu, who failed, cannot be faulted for courage. She does not mention Ikoma Hideri. She does not mention Kitsu Yue.

Kakita Kazumi went looking for the medic, in the right century and the right section of the archive, and found nothing. No testimony by anyone named Akodo Kayamayako. Not a line. Akodo Toronoko's record was there, and Akodo Kagekatsu's beside it, and the woman who spent the entire battle putting the Lion army back together is not in the archive at all. Which leaves two readings — that somebody removed her, or that she was never a person to begin with and the name was a costume. Who better to be the spy than the one everyone trusts?

Kitsu Yue's record leaves more unsaid than it says, and then it stops. The last thing in it is three lines.

I gave my name and I lost my honour in the snow. The sting is in the words the children learn. Come spring, the world changes again.

Nothing after that. Nobody anywhere says Kitsu Yue died by her own hand, and that is the form a death poem takes. Kitsu Ayoko, who had been sitting with her ancestor's scroll all evening, said the only thing she had. It is only that I do not know what she meant by this, and why she said nothing more. Kakita Kazumi wanted to go hunting through the children's verses of that era for the sting, which is not the sort of thing a hall of personal recollections holds — but the Lion cross-index their poetry by subject, meter and affect, and he knows the age to look in.

Somewhere in the Kitsu Archives Doji Setsuna stopped hearing anything said to her. It took a hand on her elbow to bring her back, and several expressions crossed her face before the reserved one arrived. Bayushi Monban walked her out. They had gone in during the afternoon; it was deep into the night, they had missed dinner, and the guards had been getting worried. He put himself between her and their curiosity and did the talking.

In the vestibule Ryu and So were asleep over a stack of bento boxes, Ryu draped protectively across them and So with a bottle of rice wine in her arms, having waited. They let them sleep and went out into a summer night that was strangely cool.

Later, in the Garden Of Virtuous Contemplation, over cold leftovers and watered wine, Shiba Midori put a theory to Kitsu Ayoko: that the reason she is not a shugenja of Midori's kind is that she has the other talent instead, the one her ancestor had, and that it would be impressive in a samurai.

Doji Setsuna knew better and said it anyway. She already said she is not a samurai. She is not of the right families. What kind of spiritualist nonsense are you peddling, to undermine the social order of Rokugan itself?

Kitsu Ayoko raised an eyebrow and waited for her to hear it. Then, evenly: she was not born samurai, her parents were peasants — and a samurai adopting her makes her samurai, which is a thing that is done, whatever anyone privately thinks of it. She excused herself not long after. What Doji Setsuna learned in that moment was not about the girl. It was that she does privately think it, and had not known she did.

She talked herself out of apologising on the grounds that an apology from someone who stands by every word is not an apology. Bayushi Monban slid her the watered wine and winked. That was a little scorpion.

Which brought them to the absence that matters. Not one record of any general or warrior present mentions that Shinjō Kamo had a Scorpion advisor. Not one. Not Matsu Sakura's. And all of them saw her.

The party worked at it and arrived somewhere colder than a conspiracy. An Imperial peace redacts treaties; it does not go through everybody's private memoirs decades later. And these memoirs are not redacted — the copies are high-fidelity, Matsu Sakura in her own sharp crisp diction, Matsu Morozane in his blunt straightforward one, nothing added, everything consistent with what the four of them lived — with one thing consistently left out. Because to write down that the Scorpion nearly cost them the battle is to write down we would have won if we had not been sabotaged, and for a clan that lives on how it looks in its own histories, that is whining. So nobody wrote it. Not as a cover-up. As a collective decision to be silent, taken separately by everyone who could have spoken.

So they set their course. The quartermasters' books, because stores are counted and a poisoning leaves a hole in a ledger that no silence closes. Shiba Midori found the treaty in the military library — Kitsu Ayoko walked up and handed it to her — and it is a certified copy taken from the Imperial originals at Otosan Uchi, carrying four chops: Miya Mimoka, Matsu Sakura, Shinjō Kamo, and Shosuro Amane. A Scorpion had no standing to witness that document. There was already an Imperial witness on it. Her chop is there anyway.

And Bayushi Monban went hunting for a map, and for the Scorpion he knows is in this castle. She took him back down the Path of Splinters and fanned herself and listened while he told her the Lion archive has no Scorpion in it anywhere. An entire major battle missing from the archives of the clan most concerned with military history. It stinks of a cover-up, and not a very well-orchestrated one.

What he wanted from her was time — days of it, stalling Kitsuki Kaage with parties and paperwork — because he knows where Ikoma Hideri's lost history is, and he intends to ride for the Snow Plain and bring it back. Something lost has been found. She pointed out that her instructions are that the Scorpion must prevail, that he was asking her to stall for weeks while he chased ghosts across a war zone, and that he did not appear to have done basic research on the battlefield he proposed to cross. My eyes glow at her. I have breathed the air.

The map settled it. The Snow Plain is about as far from the Castle of the Swift Sword as Otosan Uchi is — and Otosan Uchi has straight clan roads and the Snow Plain has a war. Three days is enough to reach either and come back from neither.

Doji Setsuna's answer to that was the simplest thing anybody said all night. They do not need to have the evidence. They need to give Kitsuki Kaage the call and let him choose. If you want to make the call today, this is the call — that the Scorpion's claim fails unless something much better is produced. If you want better confidence in that call, buy us the time. Making the decision his rather than theirs is the point, because he is the one who has to make it.

Setsuna

She has now read the archive of a battle she was inside, and the mismatch is not what she expected.

The accounts are genuine. The copies are faithful — Matsu Sakura's crisp diction, Matsu Morozane's bluntness, nothing inserted, everything consistent with what she lived through. She went in expecting a revision and found that nobody revised anything. They simply all, separately, declined to write down the same thing. Writing the Scorpion cost us this battle means writing we would have won otherwise, and a clan that lives on how it looks in its own histories does not put that on a scroll. The silence is not an edit. It is an agreement nobody had to make.

That is worse for her case and better for her understanding. There is no forger to expose. There is a whole generation of honest men and women who each chose the same omission.

The medic is the exception, and the exception is the finding. Kitsu Somalia came round on that field and said the person who had put four wounds in her was a woman whose voice she knew, because that woman had patched her up before. Akodo Kayamayako rewarmed Ikoma Akuyaku's frozen feet and dressed Matsu Morozane's burns and caught an intruder at the general's tent. And Kakita Kazumi has searched the archive and there is no such person. A woman can be left out of a battle account out of shame. Being left out of the archive entirely is either someone's work or proof the name was a costume.

Then Kitsu Yue. She called the dead and was called a foreign sorcerer for it, and her record ends on a death poem, and Kitsu Somalia — who was standing beside her, who explained the working to her, who watched her do it — wrote a career's worth of scholarship on that technique and never once named where she first saw it. The clan credits Kitsu Somalia as its founder. Setsuna has watched the founding, and it was not hers.

And her own ancestor kept the right books. Matsu Morozane was there for duels and a banner and he wrote about carts. That is a man who worked out what actually beat them. The quartermasters' ledgers are the correct target, and the reason to give Kitsuki Kaage is arithmetic rather than honour, because a ledger is the one kind of record in this castle that nobody thought to be silent about.

One thing about herself, and she should not let it slide past. She told a girl to her face that she is not samurai and not of the right families, in a garden, at two in the morning, in front of three colleagues — and discovered in the saying of it that she believes it. Not that it was impolitic. That she believes it. Kitsu Ayoko is dreaming the same battle Setsuna is, was adopted out of a village, and had already been told once by the shrine that she was not the sort of person spirits speak to. She excused herself. She had waited all evening with a scroll she could not read the end of, and the only person who thought to bring her food was a servant.

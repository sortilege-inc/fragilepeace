Grandmother

Session 5·2025-04-08

There is honour in that — if you understand what sacrifice is necessary to maintain that fragile peace.

The Border Waystation sits on the border between the Kitsu and Akodo provinces and is administered jointly by both, on a small lake with a Shrine of the Lake Kami on it. Three Kitsu samurai keep the post and rarely see visitors: Musubi and Aoi, both of Kitsu vassal families and effectively of minor-clan standing, and Inari, the only pureblood of the three, who can state without hesitating that he stands approximately thirty-seven steps from the Kitsu line of succession. A Lion gives his ancestry to that resolution and expects it to be useful. The hosts were surprised to be handed several Emerald Magistrates at once, said the papers would be read in the morning, and recommended the lake after dark and the tea house in the meantime.

What followed in the tea house was an interview conducted as hospitality. Three provincial samurai who never get visitors put their questions in order and worked through them.

A remark on Doji Setsuna's southern upbringing, and a hope that plainer chicken and beef would suit: she answered that a narrow palate understands nothing, and that this would let her appreciate what she had been raised on. Asked for the finest meal of her life, she described one she could not remember the food of — her lord, her Scorpion mentor, and a visiting Phoenix dignitary who was Shiba Sukune, newly the Phoenix Champion. Shiba Midori had been at that table too. Doji Setsuna was new in post and spent the evening watching a master class in saying a thing without saying it, and everyone leaving satisfied and certain they had played their best hand.

Asked where magistrates call home, Shiba Midori said home is where family is, and that these magistrates are as much family to her as her own adoptive family — and looked at Bayushi Monban the way the Moon Maiden used to look at him when he came to her defence. For a moment he had the Moon Maiden's look in his eyes and both of them remembered her as though she were in the room. She is the sister they are missing. He said he was also missing Lady Tsubasa, which was news, and that this was the first time since the Topaz Championship that any of them had sat down long enough to talk. I have been so many places. I have never felt this alone.

Asked whom he most trusted, Bayushi Monban did not hesitate: I trust my fellow magistrates implicitly. We are beyond clan colours. We are justice, and justice has no boundaries, no divisions.

Then the question with a hook in it, put to Doji Setsuna as a scholar of law: it is often said that justice is whatever the man of greatest standing says it is — is that true, or does truth win out? She would not go anywhere near where it was leading. Which man has greater standing than the Emperor? And does the Emperor not derive judgment from the kami? Told that the Emperor is not present at every magistrate's hearing, she answered that a magistrate is the presence of the Emperor. Bayushi Monban finished it — where a magistrate speaks, you hear the voice of the Emperor — and gave them the bonsai: every case a leaf or a branch, too much trimming and the tree dies, too little and it becomes a bush. Shiba Midori added the part that was meant for the hosts: the people of this outpost look up to them as they are ordained above them, and the duty runs both ways.

Doji Setsuna took the most idealistic position available and stayed on it. She does not privately agree with all of it. She judged the tea house neither the place nor the audience, and let it stand as something said in her presence.

Asked for the most beautiful place in Rokugan, Shiba Midori named the Shrine to Amaterasu in Centipede country and Bayushi Monban agreed with her at length. Doji Setsuna named a bluff on the coast near where she grew up, at sunset, the waves breaking far below and the mist and the smell of them coming up — where her husband had taken her. It is nearly the only time she has spoken of Shinjō Harunobu aloud, and Bayushi Monban noticed.

Asked how a magistrate's duties sit with being a good wife: I think my husband and I are well matched in that regard. We both have duties that call us elsewhere more often than not, and it makes the time together more meaningful.

Asked whether it is better to have lived a life of great accomplishment without being an exemplar of Bushidō, or to be an exemplar with less to show — the Lion Clan know their own answer and were asking for hers — she said her thinking had changed since she first heard it. She would like to keep the honour of herself and her family and her lineage, but there are deeds so consequential that one might risk even that. I am grateful that I have not yet been put in a position where I have had to choose. Shiba Midori called the question a false dichotomy: when belief and action are in accord, one can do great things.

Asked about art, Doji Setsuna weighed the stakes — three very junior functionaries on the safest internal border in the Empire — and answered honestly for once. She is partial to pillow books, particularly the outlandish ones, and told them the plot of one about a boy switched at birth who did not know he was a prince, and the young samurai who aided him on the road, and how close the two became. Shiba Midori offered haiku by the master Hanzin, a sequence on the shortness of life — lives like the wings of a moth burned away by the fire — and could not say why they move her so. Bayushi Monban's mother and brother work for a production company, so he has seen a great deal of singing and dancing and storytelling, and he values the arts a person has to be brave to attempt: to hear somebody's lament on loss, or a dance of love — that moves me. Then, laughing: even a Scorpion has the soul of a poet. Do not tell anybody. Inari spoke for the arts that take patience and years of work, which brought the talk to tattooing, at which Bayushi Monban scratched his ribs.

Then the last question, and the Kitsu are mediums between the living and the dead, though Inari was born without the talent: which of your ancestors would you most like to meet? Shiba Midori named Kami Shiba, to thank him for kneeling to a mortal for the good of the Empire. Bayushi Monban said he would meet his mother again. Doji Setsuna said she had never had the chance to speak much with her grandmother, who was Matsu and became Doji.

A cold wind came through the tea house at that exact moment, in high summer, and put the hair up on the back of nobody's neck but Shiba Midori's. Inari said it was mountain air. Shiba Midori read it: a supernatural presence, definitely there, and standing over Doji Setsuna — and for one moment, the clank of armour. Then it was gone. Doji Setsuna felt only the cold and her own skin crawling, and spent a moment composing herself. She had heard nothing, and did not know there had been anything to hear.

They walked the lake to get out of earshot. Shiba Midori laid it out: a spirit that wears armour, attached to a woman who has just named a Matsu ancestor. Doji Setsuna tried to reason at it herself and got nowhere — ancestor veneration is not her practice and never was her family's, and she has no instinct for any of it. The spirit is not going to care whether you want to interact with it. It is going to want to interact with you. There is a Shrine to the Ancestors at the station. An offering could be made.

Shiba Midori asked permission before she asked anything else, and warned that speaking with one's ancestors unprepared is never a good idea. Doji Setsuna gave the most exposed answer she has given anyone: I do not know that I would be more prepared later. I do not know what to prepare for. Bayushi Monban leaned in and said the formula — your words are your responsibility, your life is mine — and then listen to the Shiba. Do what you think is right. There is no fear.

She asked whether to petition her grandmother by name or to make a general offering. Shiba Midori's answer was tactical: name her, and if some other spirit answers, it will not be strengthened the way the right one would — and that itself would tell them something. They went general.

The offerings were built rather than bought. Doji Setsuna wrote a haiku, which is a form of remembering: her mother was the perfect Doji lady and most of that refinement came from her mother, which sat oddly against a Matsu upbringing, and poetry and calligraphy were among the things she knew of her grandmother for certain. Bayushi Monban folded a set of paper animals and gave her, separately, a lion with a blue mane like a crane's — I know less than the Shiba, but I do not want you to think this is not as important to me as it is to you.

At the shrine Bayushi Monban asked whether he should take off his mask. Shiba Midori told him not to — not all spirits are trustworthy — because unmasking offers a spirit the trust owed to family or to a fellow magistrate, and Doji Setsuna's ancestor is no exception. The shrine's monk shifted at hearing an ancestor described that way. Shiba Midori made the communion, burning the animals in order — the chrysanthemum first for the Emperor, the phoenix second for herself, the dragon in the middle, the blue-maned lion last because that one was for the spirit — and read Doji Setsuna's poem, and called the woman by both her names, Matsu Tomoe and Doji Tomoe, because for a Matsu that is the whole of the story.

A cold wind put out every light in the hall. Gold ginkgo leaves gathered at the base of nothing into a woman: armoured above the waist and in a courtier's hakama below, a daishō at her hip and a fan in her hand, the smell of jasmine on her and a pool of blood following wherever she walked. You are not Kitsu. Why have you called me here?

Doji Setsuna did not recognise her. She has only ever seen portraits, and the ancestor is the idealised form of the woman rather than the one who sat for them. Recognition came slowly and brought no comfort with it.

I am her yōjimbō, Bayushi Monban said, and got a look that could wither grass — which was not for him. You are so weak that you depend on another's sword to keep your breath? And then: you call yourself my descendant? — I thought I raised my daughter with more steel in her spine.

Then the reason, and it is the thing the record of this session has always been missing. Matsu Tomoe was a peace cow. She was handed over in a truce that failed, in which the Lion lost land and the Crane gained it. She traded the battlefield for the courtroom and blood and glory for go and flower arranging — and, being Matsu, she took a Doji name, which is backwards, because someone was supposed to take hers. Bayushi Monban knows the Matsu well enough to put that together while she was still talking. Nobody ever thanked her. Her family remembers her, when it remembers her at all, as the Doji courtier she was forced to invent in order to live among them. This one has never made the tour to the Hall of Ancestors to see my place of birth. This one has never offered me even the slightest bit of respect.

The company did not fold. Bayushi Monban argued her down point by point — that the fault was not Doji Setsuna's, that the Crane are not built as the Matsu are, that she has only just left the nest, the pride and the tree are not the same. Matsu Tomoe took the measure of the party and gave it back: a confused old man. A teenage mediocrity. A scarred, dead-eyed woman. And you. She said she had slain ten men by Doji Setsuna's age. Bayushi Monban said he had slain seven, and seven horses, in the Emperor's protection, before that age, and that age does not matter. Doji Setsuna said she is strong as a willow, able to take the wind of a hurricane, better to bend in a storm than break — and was told that sounded like her grandfather.

When was the last time you saw people like us together? — I make decisions that allow people to avoid the need to slay one another.

And there is honour in that, if you understand what sacrifice is necessary to maintain that fragile peace.

I am not saying all of my decisions will be bloodless. But the ones that have been consequential so far have been, and I am proud of that.

Of Shinjō Harunobu: a hostage, and so he will live. Better that he had died with honour on the field as a warrior. But that is a shame he will have to live with. — I intend to give him that opportunity back. — Good for you. And good for him.

Grandmother, I have done you a great disservice. — Good. You finally realised that part. We are at the beginning of wisdom, at least. Now, what do you intend to do about it? And when Doji Setsuna answered that she acts with the Emperor's authority: my battlefield is not your battlefield. You have never had to make a hard choice in your life. You have not had life and death, honour and dishonour thrown in your face and been made to choose. But if you serve the Emperor, you will. It will come. Which is the same question the Kitsu had asked over tea an hour earlier, and the same answer Doji Setsuna had given then — that she was grateful never to have had to choose — turned around and handed back to her as an indictment. That is probably the first right thing you have done in your life, accepting that chrysanthemum badge. Doing that, you serve a cause bigger than yourself.

Bayushi Monban opened his collar. Matsu Tomoe reached as if to touch the scar that goes all the way around his neck and it lit red and burned him without contact; he took the pain rather than cry out. The jagged edges of it showed through the cloth. Shiba Midori's own scar glowed in sympathy. You bear the touch of Jigoku. — I have walked that place, and returned whole, judged worthy. — Are you sure of that? — I have returned judged worthy by he who makes those choices. Doji Setsuna had no idea what either of them was talking about.

Shiba Midori offered a poem of her own and it was refused: that poem had nothing to do with me and everything to do with you. It was pretty, but that is the extent of it. What do they teach you in your home about honouring ancestors? Then the rest of it — that the party is compromised and should not be allowed to sway her from the righteous path; that Bayushi Monban is unclean and may never be clean, and must be cleansed regardless; and that Shiba Midori must cleanse herself, for having approached the dead with impurity and a transaction in hand rather than reverence. She excused the others of that charge: they had reverence. It was in the wrong place. I am not some spirit to be commanded.

Afterwards Shiba Midori confirmed what the leaves had been: the same presence from the tea house, beyond doubt. Which leaves either that Matsu Tomoe meant only the invocation — or that she had been near her granddaughter without knowing whose granddaughter she was, and could only become present once Doji Setsuna came into Lion country. Tonight was the first time anyone had offered her anything.

I will ensure that you are better remembered, grandmother. I will ensure that your sacrifice was not in vain.

What she was left with: you can remember that the eyes of all of your ancestors are upon you, and behave accordingly. You must learn to make the right choice when it is inconvenient, unpleasant, or even disadvantageous. You must put honour before everything. Nothing less should be expected of one of my descendants. And, fading: while you walk on the lands of the bones of your ancestors, know that I will walk with you, and I will be watching you. Keep a straight spine and show them you know the meaning of honour.

Bayushi Monban told the shrine monk to say nothing of what he had seen or heard. The monk looked for something defiant and did not find the courage for it, nodded, and offered the only counsel he had: I do not know what you did to offend her. I strongly suggest you do what she said to fix it.

Setsuna

Three things came out of that shrine and all three are hers to carry.

She has an ancestor walking with her through Lion ancestral lands, watching her specifically. That is protection and it is supervision, and Matsu Tomoe did not distinguish between them. The standing instruction is honour first, and it was delivered as a correction rather than as advice.

She was handed her own words back as an indictment inside the hour. At tea she said she was grateful never to have had to choose between honour and consequence. At the shrine her grandmother told her that a servant of the Emperor will be made to choose, and soon. Nobody in that hall knew she had said it earlier. It still landed like a verdict.

And she has learned what was done to her grandmother, which is the thing to act on. Matsu Tomoe was the price of a truce — a Matsu handed to the Crane in a peace that failed and lost the Lion land, made to take a Doji name when a Matsu's name is supposed to be the one that survives, and remembered by her own family only as the courtier she had to counterfeit. She was never thanked. Doji Setsuna swore to have her better remembered and to make the sacrifice mean something, which is a promise she made without knowing what it would take.

The first step is the obvious one and nobody suggested it: find that treaty and read it. She is a scholar of law who has just been told that a document in the Imperial record ate her grandmother. She is also, at this moment, riding toward a war to broker exactly such a peace — and she has been told to her face, by the woman who paid for the last one, that there is honour in preventing slaughter if you understand what sacrifice is necessary.

She also owes a debt at the Hall of Ancestors that she has never paid, now named aloud in front of two companions and a terrified monk. It is the only instruction she was given plainly enough to act on immediately.

And her yōjimbō is tainted by Jigoku — confirmed by an ancestor rather than guessed at — and under an order to be cleansed that his own ancestor doubts can be carried out. Bayushi Monban has not explained the scar, and answered it with a claim about being judged worthy that Doji Setsuna could not follow at all. He spent the evening defending her to a spirit that could have unmade him for it. One monk saw the whole of it, was asked to be quiet, and agreed — after giving the only advice in the room that was any use.

Avalanche of Battle's End

Session 14·2025-06-24

A commander who could not hear a challenge, a bodyguard who answered one in two voices at once, and a hillside that came down and ended the argument before either army could finish making it.

Akodo Kagekatsu's flank went in from the side and took the Central Hill and its fortification. Whatever he is in a tent, in the field he delivered. Matsu Sakura flipped the army's objective the moment it was hers: hold it, and grind them down — make the Unicorn bleed for every attempt to take it back. The Unicorn wanted it back. Six rounds is a day of battle, and three were already spent.

Akodo Toronoko did not fight for it so much as refuse to let anyone else. Caught in open snow — poor ground for archers, and she knew it — with spearmen coming at her flank at a run, she reinforced, and spent what the water gave her on freezing the enemy leader's cohort where it stood. Then she had the rear rank of her archers loose high and the front rank loose flat, so both volleys arrived together as one falling curtain, and the Unicorn stopped rather than walk into it. They had orders not to spend men they did not need to spend. My job is not to fight you, was how she put it. It is to be annoying.

Up on the ridge there were no war fans to read. Matsu Morozane handed the fighting to Kitsu Somalia — he had picked the men for the mountains and the mountains were behind them — and she took it and set the method: both units on one enemy at a time, break it, move to the next. The Unicorn had three cohorts to their two and fewer men in them. Whoever carried the Lion plan out of that camp had carried the route and not the size of what was coming down it.

Kitsu Somalia called Fire From Within — the Kitsu Wanderer's trick of turning one's own aggression into flame — and put it into the shugenja's cohort rather than the one in front of her. He has done that to me, or close enough, before. I am returning the favour. The cohort went up. What was left of it was one step from breaking, and its commander had to spend his turn holding men together while they burned.

Matsu Morozane gave a Rallying Cry with Shigo no Chinmoku covering his back, shouting his ashigaru forward in the most conspicuous position on that ridge — and a Unicorn officer rode out of the line on the right and screamed at him to come out and finish what he had started. Matsu Morozane recognised him. It was the man who had beaten him in the clash two days before, on a different horse.

He did not accept. Declining a challenge in the open costs seven glory, and honour and glory are social things that require witnesses willing to say what they saw — so he set Shigo no Chinmoku on the horse and behaved as though he had not heard a word of it. I am sorry, I cannot hear you over the sound of my lion mauling your face. The new horse was not the horse the officer was used to. It bolted, and he went with it, indignant, shouting at his own mount. Whether anybody who mattered was watching is a question Matsu Morozane elected to leave to a later version of himself.

Then a different cohort sent a man forward to the front of his line, blade out, to call the coward flinging fire instead of fighting like a man. Come out and fight, coward. You want to throw fire? I have fire for you right here. He meant Kitsu Somalia, and he was a bushi who did not know he was challenging a shugenja, or a woman. Kitsu Yue asked to take it in her commander's place, and Kitsu Somalia heard the asking twice over, in the voice of the woman standing at her elbow and in the voice of the descendant looking out through her. Only as honour allows, Kitsu Somalia said, and let her go.

It took one exchange. The man came apart under his own fury before he could land anything, left an opening, and Kitsu Yue put Utaku Mangiano on the ground with a single blow — the second of the three Unicorn leaders on that ridge to go down. His cohort, already close to breaking, took the panic of it.

Below, Akodo Toronoko swapped from holding to hurting — Righteous Example, and she picked up a bow herself and put arrows into the squares in front of her, which took five measures of attrition and five of panic out of the Unicorn army and made every other Lion leader's work easier for a turn.

Two of the three Unicorn leaders on that ridge were now down, which left the shugenja commanding. Kitsu Somalia was carrying too much strife to give orders cleanly and unmasked to clear it, which for her looks like a woman on a battlefield complaining about her blackened nails.

The Unicorn spent everything they had left in one turn. Breath of the Fire Dragon across the Lion line to drive it into mass panic — and the shugenja himself riding to the front of his people with a blazing sword in each hand. Do you dare fight the kami's chosen?

Matsu Morozane heard that one. He went straight for a heart-piercing strike and missed it, and the missing compromised him, and a compromised man in a clash can be finished. He was knocked off his horse and left on the ground burning a hole in the snow, in front of his own troops, dying.

Kitsu Somalia was not far. She tried Extinguish and it failed and she had no Void left to spend on it. So she threw everything into one improvised working — a Hail Mary, on the understanding that if it did not land he was gone — and Path to Inner Peace needed four successes out of her and gave five. He stopped dying. He was still severely wounded, still bleeding, and still on fire, and she was only going to get one of those, so she took the fire off him.

And then the hill came down. An avalanche off the Central Hill split the field in two and ended the day. The idea of it had been in the air on the Lion side for a turn — a great deal of unsettled snow on a slope, a great many explosions under it, and a Kitsu standing there who could ask the water kami for things. Whether they were asked or simply obliged, the memory does not settle.

When it stopped, all three leaders of the force the Unicorn had sent to break the Lion flank were dead with most of their troops, which was a disaster for that side of the field. The Lion held the Central Hill, which is what they had been ordered to do. They had one commander coming back on a stretcher who should not have been coming back at all.

Setsuna

The Lion held the hill. The force sent to break their flank was destroyed to its last commander. And Ikoma Ichigo told her, in the Hall of Scribes, that the Snow Plain was a Unicorn victory and a Lion defeat.

Both can be true, and the gap between them is the thing worth having. There are days of this left. An army that holds a hill on one evening can lose everything by the next, and how the Lion lost what they were standing on is what the record has now been quiet about twice. She should stop treating the historian's summary as the fact of the battle and start treating it as the last line of a story whose middle is missing.

The ending is evidence in a way nobody fighting there could have cared about. The engagement did not conclude — it was buried. An avalanche came off the Central Hill and cut the field in half. Snow moves ground, and ground that moved three hundred years ago is not where an old survey says it is. Whatever boundary the mines were described by before that winter, the hill the line was drawn on came down that winter.

Two things she should be honest with herself about, because both are hers.

Her own ancestor was formally challenged in front of two armies and pretended not to hear it, and set an animal on the man's horse to cover the pretence. It cost him glory he was gambling nobody could witness. That is not the conduct any Lion account of this battle is going to record, and she now knows it happened, and she is the one arguing in a courtroom that the Lion record is incomplete. It is incomplete in both directions.

And Kitsu Yue asked permission to die for her commander and was heard in stereo — the woman there and the woman looking through her. Whatever this memory is, it is not only a memory. If the ancestors at the Snow Plain can be heard by the people who carry them, then the question of what happened on that field is not purely an archival one, and Kitsu Taigen was right to be frightened of what his daughter was hearing.
